Sparse testing and multi-environment genomic prediction in a structured Miscanthus breeding population

Abstract
Genetic improvement of Miscanthus, a high-yielding perennial C4 bioenergy grass, is constrained by genotype × environment interactions (G×E) and high phenotyping costs in multi-environment trials. Sparse testing combined with genomic prediction could make these trials more cost-efficient by allowing more genotypes to be evaluated within a fixed budget. We evaluated 125 genotypes from three population groups for dry matter yield (DM), fresh weight (FW) and moisture content (MC) across five site-year environments. Three models with population group as a fixed effect were tested under two breeding scenarios, CV1 (untested genotypes) and CV2 (genotypes tested in other environments), and in sparse-testing designs varying the proportions of overlapping (OL) and non-overlapping (NOL) genotypes. To assess the effect of population structure, predictive ability was also evaluated within population groups, using one phenotype per genotype per site, adjusted for training-set group means. Predictive ability was higher under CV2 than CV1, reflecting information from other environments, including other years at the same site. Incorporating G×E generally improved predictive ability, particularly for DM and FW. For CV1, predictive ability decreased from 0.50–0.54 across groups to 0.08–0.27 within groups, whereas for CV2, within-group predictive ability remained moderate for DM and FW (0.39–0.46) and high for MC (0.71–0.72). In sparse-testing designs, predictive ability was more strongly associated with the number of unique training genotypes than with the proportions of NOL and OL genotypes. These results support phenotyping more unique genotypes and show that within-group predictive ability is higher for candidates phenotyped at another site.

Introduction
Miscanthus is a high-yielding C₄ perennial grass widely used for lignocellulosic biomass production, with applications in renewable energy and greenhouse gas mitigation (Hastings et al., 2008; Robson et al., 2013). It is well adapted to diverse environments and can be cultivated on marginal soils with low inputs (Boehmel et al., 2008). To date, commercial production has relied predominantly on a single triploid clone of the nothospecies, Miscanthus × giganteus (Mxg), a naturally occurring hybrid between Miscanthus sacchariflorus and Miscanthus sinensis (Clark et al., 2019; Hodkinson & Renvoize, 2001). Developing improved varieties with higher yield, biomass quality, and resilience to abiotic stress across diverse environments remains a key breeding objective (Clifton-Brown et al., 2019a, 2019b). 
Genetic improvement of yield and resilience traits, which are quantitatively inherited, is slow and exacerbated by the perennial nature of the crop. Conventional Miscanthus breeding requires multiple years due to extended generation intervals and the delayed expression of important traits such as yield, quality, and resilience. For example, biomass yield and quality are typically assessed once the plant has reached full maturity, which occurs after a growth period of approximately three years in the UK (Clifton‐Brown et al., 2019). 
Genotype × environment interaction (G×E) further complicates selection of promising genotypes (Crossa et al., 2006). The performance of genotypes evaluated across environments depends on the interaction between genetic properties and environmental conditions, resulting in differences and rank changes among genotypes (Cooper & DeLacy, 1994). Such inconsistency in genotype performance across locations and years reduces the reliability of selection decisions and can limit realized genetic gain. Multi-environment trials (METs) are therefore essential to estimate breeding values and assess genotype stability across target environments. However, evaluating genotypes in METs is expensive, and early-stage selection is often less reliable because it is based on data from a single location using unreplicated trials. This provides limited information on G×E and increases the risk of discarding genotypes with superior adaptive potential (Jarquín et al., 2017). 
Despite these advances, the cost of phenotyping across environments remains a major limitation (Cobb et al., 2019). Sparse testing has emerged as a strategy to reduce phenotyping costs by evaluating genotypes in only a subset of environments while maintaining connectivity through shared genotypes and genomic relationships (Jarquin et al., 2020). This allows unobserved genotype × environment combinations to be predicted and more genotypes to be evaluated within a fixed budget. Although sparse testing has been widely studied in annual crops, its application in perennial systems such as Miscanthus remains limited. The optimal balance between overlapping (i.e., tested in all environments, OL) and non-overlapping (i.e., tested in a single environment, NOL) genotypes is also not well established. The present panel comprises M. sinensis, M. sacchariflorus and interspecific hybrids, which differ in mean dry matter yield (DM), fresh weight (FW) and moisture content (MC). In such structured populations, predictive ability computed across all genotypes may partly reflect differences among population groups and therefore overstate the ability to rank genotypes within groups, which is particularly relevant for selection within a breeding programme (Guo et al., 2014; Olatoye et al., 2020; Windhausen et al., 2012). In Miscanthus, subpopulation structure accounted for much of the genomic prediction accuracy within M. sinensis and M. sacchariflorus diversity panels (Olatoye et al., 2020). Furthermore, because Miscanthus is perennial, the same plants are measured in successive years, so a genotype predicted in one year may already have been measured in another year at the same site. Within-site genetic correlations between measurements of the same trait in multiple years can be high. Thus, this is a favourable factor for phenotypic or genomic prediction of traits in years when traits are not measured. The objectives of this study were to (i) assess the impact of modelling G×E on predictive ability for phenotypes measured in multiple years in two field trials, under two breeding scenarios: prediction of genotypes not tested in other field trials, and prediction of genotypes with varying representation in other trials; (ii) evaluate the effect of different proportions of OL and NOL genotypes on predictive ability; and (iii) determine the extent to which predictive ability reflects differences among population groups rather than the ranking of genotypes within groups.
Materials and Methods
Plant material and environments 
Field experiments were conducted near Aberystwyth, UK, at the Institute of Biological, Environmental and Rural Sciences (IBERS; Aber), and near Braunschweig, Germany, at the Julius Kühn-Institut (JKI; Brau; 52°17′56.3″N, 10°26′6.6″E). At Aberystwyth, genotypes were drawn from the ABR33 field trial (52°25′57.7″N, 4°01′33.2″W), established between 2012 and 2014 with 953 Miscanthus accessions propagated by rhizome division from previously established European trials near Aberystwyth, Catania (Italy) and Braunschweig, and planted in a randomised complete block design with three blocks at 1.5 × 1.5 m spacing (Iacono et al., 2023). Accessions with recorded collection sites originated from south-east China, South Korea, Taiwan and Japan, spanning elevations from sea level to 3,000 m (Huang et al., 2019).
A total of 125 genotypes present at both sites, comprising M. sinensis (n = 47), M. sacchariflorus (n = 56) and interspecific hybrids (n = 22), were used in this study, although not all genotypes were phenotyped in every environment. Phenotypic data were collected across five site × year environments: Aberystwyth in 2015 and 2016, and Braunschweig in 2014, 2015 and 2016.

SNP genotyping and quality control
Genotypic data were obtained from a previous study of the ABR33 trial (Iacono et al., 2023). Briefly, DNA was extracted from leaf samples of the 953 genotypes surviving in spring 2015, and RAD-seq genotyping was performed as described by Slavov et al. (2014). Because the panel comprised several Miscanthus species, not all of which had a reference genome available at the time of genotyping, SNPs were called using the UNEAK pipeline suitable for species with no reference genome (Lu et al., 2013), yielding 25,014 SNPs with a minor allele frequency of at least 0.0025 and a call rate of at least 80%.
Genotypic data for the 125 genotypes used in the present study were extracted from this dataset. SNPs were coded as the number of copies of the minor allele (0, 1 and 2 for homozygous major, heterozygous and homozygous minor genotypes, respectively) and processed using PLINK 1.9 (Chang et al., 2015); ploidy-specific allele dosage was therefore not represented. Markers with more than 20% missing values, a minor allele frequency below 5% or heterozygosity greater than 95% within the 125-genotype subset were excluded, leaving 13,687 SNPs. Remaining missing genotypes were imputed using the mean genotype at each marker. The genomic relationship matrix (G) was computed following VanRaden (2008) using the A.mat function in rrBLUP (Endelman, 2011). Because the marker data were supplied using 0/1/2 rather than the −1/0/1 coding expected by the function, the G used in all prediction analyses was based on 11,901 of the 13,687 SNPs. A matrix computed from all 13,687 SNPs with the expected coding was nearly identical (r = 0.999 for off-diagonal elements) and changed predictive ability by at most 0.033; this matrix was used for the variance components in Figure 2. 
Genetic groups
Population structure was assessed on the 11,901 SNPs used to compute G (Section 2.3). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using SNPRelate (Zheng et al., 2012), and the first two principal components were plotted (Figure 1a). Genotypes were assigned to genetic clusters with the find.clusters function of adegenet (Jombart, 2008), using k-means clustering on the first 50 principal components; three clusters were identified from the Bayesian information criterion (Figure S1), and the number was fixed at three in all subsequent analyses. These clusters, corresponding to M. sacchariflorus (n = 56), M. sinensis (n = 47) and interspecific hybrids (n = 22), were used as the population groups in all models.

Sparse testing designs
We investigated the feasibility of implementing sparse testing in the Miscanthus breeding programme. With 125 genotypes available, we varied the proportions of NOL and OL genotypes across five environments (site × year combinations). For the fully non-overlapping design (D1; 100% NOL), the 125 genotypes were randomly partitioned into five non-overlapping sets of 25 genotypes, with each set assigned to one environment. Thus, each genotype was evaluated in only one environment. For the remaining designs (D2–D6), the overlapping and non-overlapping genotype pools were drawn using simple random sampling according to the proportions shown in Table 1. Random assignment was performed independently for each design and replicate.
Table 1. Sparse testing design across five environments (site × year combinations)
For D1, 25 unique genotypes were evaluated in each environment, with no genotypes shared across environments. Thus, all 125 unique genotypes were represented in the training set, with 25 evaluated and 100 untested genotypes within each environment. For example, in D2, five OL genotypes were evaluated across all five environments, while 20 NOL genotypes were evaluated in only one environment. The 125 genotype × environment evaluations therefore comprised 25 evaluations of OL genotypes (5 × 5) and 100 evaluations of NOL genotypes (20 × 5). The training set contained 125 genotype × environment combinations, while the prediction set contained the remaining 500 combinations. Across D1–D6, the proportions of NOL genotypes were 100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, 20%, and 0%, respectively, while the numbers of unique genotypes represented in the training set were 125, 105, 85, 65, 45, and 25, respectively. Each D1–D6 design was independently replicated 10 times. The same three prediction models (M1–M3) were fitted to each design, and predictive ability was calculated as the Pearson correlation between predicted values and genotype BLUEs within the same environment. All prediction models were fitted using the BGLR package (Pérez & de los Campos, 2014).
Model assessment using cross-validation schemes
We calculated predictive ability under two prediction strategies commonly used by breeders: CV1 and CV2. CV1 predicts the performance of newly developed genotypes, which have not been tested in any field trials. CV2 simulates a breeding scenario under sparse testing, where all genotypes are tested in at least one, but not all, environments (Burgueño et al., 2012). Random five-fold cross-validation was used in both scenarios, and predictive ability was calculated as the Pearson correlation between predicted values and genotype BLUEs within the same environment. Genotype BLUEs were obtained as predictions averaged with equal weight over years (2015–2016 at Aberystwyth and 2014–2016 at Braunschweig) and therefore retain differences among population groups. The proportion of variation in the trial-level BLUEs explained by population group was quantified by the coefficient of determination (R²) of a linear regression of BLUEs on population group within each trial.
Population group adjustment. 
Two sets of phenotypes were analysed with identical data partitions: the trial-level BLUEs (population group unadjusted) and BLUEs adjusted for population group means (population group adjusted). For the adjusted phenotypes, within each cross-validation partition the mean BLUE of each trial × population group was calculated from the training genotypes only and subtracted from all phenotypes in that trial × population group, in both the training and validation sets. Training group means were added back to the predictions.
Prediction models. 
Models M1–M3 were fitted as described above, except that environment represented trial rather than site-year and population group was accounted for through the phenotypes rather than as a fixed effect. Group differences were either retained (unadjusted) or removed using training-set group means (adjusted); the adjustment is analogous to fitting population group as a fixed effect using training data only, so line and genomic effects are estimated from within-group variation. Adjusted and unadjusted analyses used identical partitions, priors and random seeds. Scaled inverse-χ² priors with 5 degrees of freedom were used, with prior modes of 10% of the training-set phenotypic variance for random effects and 50% for the residual. Models were run for 8,000 iterations, with the first 2,000 discarded as burn-in and a thinning interval of 5.
Cross-validation. 
CV1 and CV2 were applied as described above, but across the two trials: in CV1, all records of a genotype were masked at both trials, and in CV2, a genotype’s phenotype at one trial was predicted using its phenotype at the other, with the one genotype observed at only one trial always kept in the training set. Each scheme used five folds and 10 repetitions, and the same partitions were used for all traits, models and phenotype sets.
Evaluation. 
Within each repetition, predictions from all folds were pooled and evaluated separately for each trial. Across-group predictive ability was the Pearson correlation between predicted values and BLUEs across all genotypes. Pooled within-group predictive ability was the same correlation after centring both predicted values and BLUEs on their population group means. This measures only the ranking of genotypes within groups; centring was applied only at evaluation. Results are means over the 10 repetitions. Convergence and Monte Carlo variability were assessed using independent chains and longer runs, confirming the stability of the reported predictive ability estimates.
Results
Phenotypic summary and broad-sense heritability
Notable variation in phenotypic values was observed for DM, FW and MC (Table 4). Mean phenotypic values ranged from 0.27 kg to 2.70 kg, 0.34 kg to 4.04 kg, and 16.17% to 31.66% for DM, FW, and MC, respectively. Phenotypic values also differed among population groups (one-way ANOVA within each environment, P ≤ 0.003 in 14 of 15 trait × environment combinations; Figure 1b): interspecific hybrids had the highest DM and FW in every environment, whereas M. sacchariflorus had the lowest median MC in every environment. The H2 for the three traits varied from 0.70 to 0.80, 0.75 to 0.84, and 0.52 to 0.92 for DM, FW, and MC, respectively. This reflects the influences of genetic and environmental factors as well as their interactions across different locations and years. Because population group was fitted as a fixed effect, these H2 estimates exclude differences among population groups; with population group fitted as random and its variance included in the denominator, H2 ranged from 0.33 to 0.58, 0.32 to 0.62, and 0.33 to 0.81 for DM, FW and MC, respectively (Table 4).

Population genetic structure
The analysis of population genetic structure in the Miscanthus genotypes revealed clear differentiation patterns (Figure 1a). Both PCA and k-means clustering (three clusters, selected using the Bayesian information criterion; Figure S1) revealed a genetic structure that corresponded with the three sub-populations. Along the first principal component (PC1), which accounted for 22.57% of the total variation, Miscanthus sinensis, M. sacchariflorus, and their interspecific hybrids were clearly separated. M. sinensis genotypes clustered on the right side of the scatter plot, M. sacchariflorus on the left, and the interspecific hybrids occupied an intermediate position (Figure 1a).

Variance Components 
To understand the sources of variability influencing trait performance across environments, we used mixed linear models to estimate variance components. The relative percentage of variance explained by the different model terms is shown in Figure 2, with population group fitted as a random effect so that its contribution could be quantified. Population group accounted for 21–32% of the total variance across traits and models, although this component was estimated from only three groups and varied among replicate fits. Including G×E effects reduced the variability captured by the residual and environment terms. Relative to M1, including G×E in M3 decreased residual variance by 50% for DM, 59% for FW, and 45% for MC. Environmental variance decreased by 15% for DM, 20% for FW, and 13% for MC, though this component was estimated with substantially more uncertainty across replicate fits than the residual-variance reduction, reflecting the limited number of environments (five) available to estimate it. Among the three traits, FW showed the greatest proportion of G×E variance in M3 (16.9% of total variance), followed by MC (14.5%) and DM (13.2%). Genomic main-effect variance in M3 was highest for MC (11.2%), compared with 6.4% for FW and 5.7% for DM, while genotype main-effect variance was highest for MC (11.0%), compared with 7.5% for DM and 6.9% for FW. These patterns indicate that FW had the largest relative contribution of G×E variance among the three traits, whereas MC had the largest proportion of its total variance attributable to genomic main effects (Figure 2).

Predictive ability under CV1 and CV2
Under CV1, we observed that the models with markers (M2 and M3) had higher predictive ability than M1, which had only phenotypic information. The inclusion of the G×E term in M3 further increased predictive ability for most trait–environment combinations compared to the main-effect models M1 and M2, with the exception of moisture content at Brau14 and Brau16, where M2 performed marginally better than M3. The predictive ability ranged from −0.63 to 0.91, −0.56 to 0.88, and −0.60 to 0.79 for DM, FW, and MC, respectively (Figure 3a, Table S1). 
Under CV2, predictive ability was generally moderate to high. For DM and FW, M3 generally showed higher predictive ability than the main-effect models, although M2 performed marginally better for some environment–trait combinations. For MC, differences among models were generally smaller, and no model consistently showed the highest predictive ability. The predictive ability ranged from 0.09 to 0.95, 0.00 to 0.95, and 0.00 to 0.94 for DM, FW, and MC, respectively (Figure 3b, Table S1).

Predictive performance under sparse testing designs
To evaluate the effectiveness of sparse testing in the Miscanthus breeding programme, we compared the predictive ability of the main-effect models (M1 and M2) with the interaction model (M3). Overall, we observed a similar trend in predictive ability across all tested configurations (i.e. percentages of NOL vs OL genotypes across environments), with only minor variations. Predictive ability was generally highest under configurations with a high proportion of NOL genotypes and lowest under the completely OL configuration for all traits (Figure 4). The interaction model M3 exhibited the highest predictive ability for DM and FW at every configuration, while for MC, M2 performed comparably to or better than M3 at most configurations. Predictive ability ranged from 0.25 to 0.69 for DM, 0.19 to 0.62 for FW, and 0.29 to 0.61 for MC (Figure 4, Table S2).
To isolate the effect of overlap (NOL/OL) from the number of unique training genotypes, which co-vary in the D1–D6 series (nominally 125 unique genotypes at 100% NOL, declining to 25 at 100% OL, with total genotype × environment combinations fixed at 125), we constructed designs using all pairwise, triplet, and quadruplet combinations of the five environments (five replicates each), holding the number of unique training genotypes fixed within each subset size (40, 36, and 32 genotypes for two-, three-, and four-environment subsets, respectively) while varying the proportion shared across environments from 0% to 100%. Predictive ability remained narrowly bounded within each subset size (0.43–0.44, 0.41–0.43, and 0.38–0.44 for two, three, and four environments, respectively), and its correlation with overlap proportion (r = 0.34, 0.75, and 0.62) was consistently weaker than the correlation between predictive ability and number of unique training genotypes in the D1–D6 series (r = 0.98, using either the nominal or the realised numbers of unique training genotypes). This indicates that the number of unique training genotypes, rather than the NOL/OL structure itself, contributed substantially to predictive-ability differences among the sparse-testing designs evaluated (Figure S2, Table S3).

Population structure and within-group predictive ability
Population structure and correlations between years and trials
Population group accounted for 22–34% of the variation among genotype BLUEs within each trial, with values of 34% and 30% for DM, 30% and 26% for FW, and 22% and 23% for MC at Aberystwyth and Braunschweig, respectively. Site-year BLUEs were strongly correlated between years at Aberystwyth (r = 0.77–0.90) and less consistently at Braunschweig (r = 0.16–0.75; Figure S3; Table S4). Trial-level BLUEs were correlated between Aberystwyth and Braunschweig (r = 0.59, 0.55 and 0.78 for DM, FW and MC, respectively), and correlations remained moderate to high after centering on population group means (r = 0.48, 0.44 and 0.72, respectively).
Across-group and within-group predictive ability
For untested genotypes (CV1), across-group predictive ability of M3 ranged from 0.50 to 0.54 across the three traits (Table S5), whereas pooled within-group predictive ability was substantially lower, ranging from 0.19 to 0.31 for unadjusted phenotypes and from 0.08 to 0.27 for population group-adjusted phenotypes, with the lowest value observed for DM at Aberystwyth (Figure 5). For genotypes phenotyped at the other trial (CV2), across-group predictive ability was 0.59–0.68 for DM and FW and 0.76–0.79 for MC, while adjusted within-group predictive ability remained moderate for DM and FW (0.39–0.46) and high for MC (0.71–0.72; Figure 6). Under CV2, M1, which uses no marker information, reached 0.25–0.40 for DM and FW and 0.67–0.70 for MC, with M3 improving on M1 by 0.06–0.14 and 0.02–0.04, respectively (Table S5). Population group adjustment resulted in similar or slightly lower within-group predictive ability than the unadjusted analysis (differences <0.06, except 0.11 for DM under CV1 at Aberystwyth).

Discussion
This study evaluated the potential of sparse testing combined with multi-environment genomic prediction to improve selection efficiency in Miscanthus breeding. Four key findings emerged. First, the model incorporating genotype × environment interaction (G×E; M3) generally showed higher predictive ability than the main-effect models (M1 and M2) under CV1 and CV2, with the largest improvements for DM and FW, and consistently outperformed them for DM and FW in the sparse-testing designs. Second, predictive ability was substantially higher under CV2 than CV1, reflecting phenotypic information from other environments, including other years at the same site. Third, sparse-testing designs with a high proportion of non-overlapping (NOL) genotypes generally showed higher predictive ability under a fixed phenotyping budget, a pattern that largely reflected the larger number of unique training genotypes in these designs. Fourth, when each genotype was represented by one phenotype per site, much of the across-group predictive ability for untested genotypes reflected differences among population groups. Within-group predictive ability was low for untested genotypes (CV1) and moderate (DM and FW) to high (MC) for genotypes that were also tested and phenotyped in the other field trial.

Modelling G×E improves genomic predictive ability
Including genomic information in prediction models generally improved predictive ability relative to the phenotypic baseline model M1. This was expected, as molecular markers capture realised genomic relationships among individuals rather than expected pedigree relationships, enabling the model to account for variation in sibling relationships arising from Mendelian sampling and to exploit distant relatedness that pedigree records cannot resolve (Bernardo & Yu, 2007). Lubanga et al. (2021) reported a similar advantage for marker-based models over pedigree and phenotypic approaches in tea, consistent with the broader evidence that genomic relationship matrices improve breeding value estimation across species.
The further improvement achieved by M3, which incorporates G×E (Jarquín et al., 2014), reflects the model's ability to borrow information from other environments. By modelling the interaction between marker effects and environments, M3 captures environment-specific genetic effects that M1 and M2 cannot. This is directly supported by the variance component results that including G×E in M3 reduced both residual and environmental variance relative to M1 across all three traits, indicating that a meaningful proportion of previously unexplained variation was attributable to differential genotype responses across environments. The reduction in environmental variance was most pronounced for FW, which also showed the largest proportion of variance attributable to G×E (16.9% of total variance in M3), suggesting that biomass-related traits in Miscanthus could be influenced by G×E and therefore stand to benefit most from models that account for it explicitly. 
Our results are consistent with those reported by Jarquín et al. (2017), who showed that incorporating G×E substantially improved genomic prediction accuracy for wheat yield in multi-environment trials, particularly in environments with moderate numbers of tested genotypes. Similarly, Lado et al. (2016) found that modelling G×E with unbalanced data from a wheat breeding programme improved prediction of new genotypes when information from relatives across environments was included. The present study extends this evidence to a perennial bioenergy crop with pronounced G×E, demonstrating that the M3 model is effective even with the relatively modest population sizes and limited number of environments evaluated in the current study. However, our results contrast with those reported by Widener et al. (2024), who found that incorporating G×E did not notably improve predictive ability for dry yield across a broad panel of M. sinensis and M. sacchariflorus accessions evaluated across diverse northern-hemisphere latitudes. This difference in findings may reflect differences in population composition (i.e., our study population contained genotypes from different species), environmental structure, and the relationships between training and target environments across the two studies.
Genomic prediction under CV1 and CV2 breeding schemes 
Genomic selection (GS) provides a framework to accelerate breeding by predicting the performance of untested genotypes using genome-wide markers (Meuwissen et al., 2001). By capturing realized genomic relationships, GS enables earlier selection and can improve selection accuracy (Crossa et al., 2016; Goddard et al., 2011; Heffner et al., 2009; Lubanga et al., 2023). Cross-validation strategies are valuable as they enable the assessment of prediction accuracy of models (Pérez-Cabal et al., 2012). In this study, predictive ability was generally higher under CV2 than CV1 across traits, models, and environments. This was expected because, under CV1, target genotypes have not been phenotyped in any environment, whereas under CV2, each target genotype has been observed in at least one environment. This phenotypic information allows the models to exploit correlations across environments, while marker-based models can additionally leverage genomic relationships between genotypes. The higher predictive ability under CV2 therefore reflects the additional information available from partially observed genotypes across environments. Because environments were site × year combinations, this information could include the same plants measured in another year at the same site. The site-year CV2 results therefore apply mainly to predicting missing years at a tested site rather than performance at a new site. The moderate to high broad-sense heritability estimates across all traits and environments indicate consistent expression of genetic effects relative to environmental noise, providing a favourable basis for genomic prediction. The heritability estimates are consistent with Zhao et al. (2021), where moderate to high repeatability across sites supported higher prediction accuracy under CV2 than CV1. Environments with higher heritability provided more reliable phenotypes for CV2, amplifying its advantage over CV1, which relies solely on marker-based relationships. These findings align with Jarquín et al. (2017) and Lubanga, Okaron, et al. (2025), all of whom reported higher prediction accuracy under CV2 scenarios compared to CV1 in multi-environment genomic prediction studies for wheat and groundnut respectively. For Miscanthus breeding programmes, greater benefit may be achieved by evaluating more genotypes across environments rather than concentrating phenotyping effort on a smaller subset of genotypes evaluated comprehensively in all environments.
Predictive ability under different sparse testing designs 
Sparse-testing designs differed in predictive ability, which generally decreased as the proportion of overlapping genotypes increased. While this is seemingly a counterintuitive result, increasing overlap simultaneously reduced the number of unique genotypes available for model training, from 125 under the fully NOL design to 25 under the fully overlapping design. The additional fixed-unique-genotype analysis showed that predictive ability varied relatively little when overlap was changed while the number of unique genotypes was held constant. This indicates that the decline in predictive ability across D1–D6 was substantially associated with the reduction in the number of unique training genotypes rather than with the NOL/OL structure itself. Genomic relationships among genotypes may nevertheless facilitate connectivity across environments, reducing the need for the same genotypes to be evaluated in every environment.
These results are consistent with those of Garcia-Abadillo et al. (2024) and Lubanga, Ifie, et al. (2025) who reported declining predictive ability as the proportion of overlapping genotypes increased in sugarcane and cassava populations respectively. By contrast,Persa et al. (2023) found that including 10 overlapping genotypes per environment improved soybean predictive ability, and Crespo‐Herrera et al. (2021) reported stable prediction accuracy with 30–50 shared wheat genotypes across environments. Both of those studies, however, used substantially larger training populations, between 95 and 195 genotypes per environment in the soybean study, providing greater power to estimate marker effects even after allocating entries to overlap. This suggests that the optimal balance between overlapping and non-overlapping genotypes depends on training population size and, importantly, on the number of unique genotypes represented in the training set.
Predictive ability within population groups
In structured panels, predictive ability calculated across all genotypes rewards a model for assigning genotypes to the correct population group as well as for ranking them within it (Guo et al., 2014; Windhausen et al., 2012). For untested genotypes, much of the across-group predictive ability reflected differences among population groups, and within-group predictive ability was low. Because breeders already know the population group of their candidates, the practical value of genomic prediction for selection depends largely on its ability to rank candidates within groups. Consistent with this, Olatoye et al. (2020) found that population structure alone accounted for much of the genomic prediction accuracy even within M. sinensis and M. sacchariflorus panels.
Within-group predictive ability was highest for genotypes tested and phenotyped at the other site because of the strong genetic correlations between sites. For untested genotypes, genomic prediction is the only way to rank candidates within groups, but on its own it gave low within-group predictive ability. Because Miscanthus is perennial, the same plants are measured every year. In the site-year analysis, a genotype predicted under CV2 or sparse testing is informed by the same genotype measured in another year at the same site. These results apply to predicting data for years when phenotyping was not performed, not to predicting performance at a new site. When each genotype had only one phenotype per trial, so that information could only come from the other trial, within-group predictive ability was still moderate for DM and FW and high for MC.
Reliably predicting untested genotypes within groups would likely require larger training populations within each group (Isidro y Sánchez & Akdemir, 2021), particularly for the interspecific hybrids, of which only 22 were included here. Training on the parental species is unlikely to help, because predictions of interspecific populations from M. sinensis and M. sacchariflorus panels are likely to be poor (e.g., Olatoye et al., 2020).
Practical implications for Miscanthus breeding programmes
The results have direct practical relevance for the design of multi-environment trials in Miscanthus breeding programmes. Phenotyping is a major cost constraint in Miscanthus improvement, and its perennial growth habit means that evaluation trials are maintained for several years before yield traits can be reliably assessed. Under these conditions, sparse testing with NOL designs provides a means of evaluating a larger number of genotypes across multiple environments without increasing the phenotyping budget. Fully NOL designs, with only 25 genotypes evaluated per environment (20% of the full population), maintained moderate to high across-group predictive ability. However, each genotype was predicted in the remaining four site-years, one or two of which were other years at the same site, so part of this performance reflects repeated measurements of the same plants. These results therefore primarily support reducing annual phenotyping at established trials, where genotypes do not need to be measured every year, rather than reducing testing across sites. For a perennial crop in which plots are harvested annually, omitting harvest measurements in some years could reduce phenotyping costs. For untested genotypes, predictive ability was much lower within population groups than across the whole panel. Genomic prediction was therefore more effective for candidates phenotyped in at least one environment and had limited ability to rank completely untested genotypes within a group.
Limitations of this study
The primary limitation of this study is the size of the training population. With 125 genotypes evaluated across five environments, the statistical power available to estimate marker effects and G×E variance components was constrained, and predictive abilities under CV1 were modest and variable across traits and environments. The relatively small population also limits the generalisability of the findings to broader Miscanthus germplasm. Larger training populations would be expected to improve predictions under CV1. The size of the training set is known to impact the accuracy of GP models (Alemu et al., 2024; Goddard, 2009) and often correlates positively with predictive ability (Lubanga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019). However, increasing training population size is costly, and an optimal population size for Miscanthus breeding programmes could instead be estimated to balance predictive ability and resource allocation through selective phenotyping, as suggested by Isidro y Sánchez & Akdemir (2021).
A second limitation concerns ploidy and marker dosage. SNPs were coded as diploid (0/1/2), and ploidy-specific allele dosage could not be modelled, which is particularly relevant for M. sacchariflorus and M. × giganteus, which can be tetraploid and triploid, respectively. In other polyploid systems, incorporating allele dosage has been shown to improve predictive ability, although the magnitude of improvement depends on genetic architecture and genotype frequencies (Batista et al., 2022). Future work should evaluate dosage-sensitive genotype calling against a Miscanthus-specific reference genome and the explicit incorporation of allele dosage into genomic prediction models for mixed-ploidy germplasm.
A third limitation is population structure. The panel comprised three population groups, corresponding to different species, that differed in mean performance for all traits. Thus, in contrast to among-group prediction, within-group prediction for untested genotypes was poor and probably not sufficiently accurate to be used for breeding. This limitation can be mitigated by much larger sample sizes within groups.
Finally, this study included only five site × year environments and did not incorporate explicit environmental covariates into the G×E model. The limited number of environments restricts inference about G×E and model performance beyond the environments represented in the training data. Including climatic, soil, and management variables as environmental predictors could improve the characterisation of environment-specific genetic effects and potentially improve prediction in untested environments. Future studies should therefore evaluate these approaches across a larger and more diverse set of environments and validate their performance in independent Miscanthus breeding populations.
Conclusion
This study shows that sparse testing can be effectively combined with multi-environment genomic prediction in Miscanthus breeding. Models incorporating genomic information and genotype × environment interactions generally achieved higher predictive ability under CV1 and CV2, with the clearest improvements observed for DM and FW. Predictive ability was generally higher under CV2 than CV1, reflecting the additional phenotypic information available when target genotypes have been evaluated in other environments, including other years at the same site. Within population groups, predictive ability for untested genotypes was low, whereas prediction of genotypes phenotyped at the other site remained moderate for DM and FW and high for MC. Under sparse testing, designs containing a higher proportion of non-overlapping genotypes generally achieved higher predictive ability. However, this pattern largely reflected the larger number of unique training genotypes represented in these designs rather than genotype overlap alone. Overall, sparse testing provides a practical strategy for evaluating more genotypes within a fixed phenotyping budget while retaining useful predictive ability across environments.
